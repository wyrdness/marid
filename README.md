## Overview

Marid (Arabic مَارِد, mārid, 'rebellious') is a class of powerful jinn in Islamic theology and Arabian folklore. The term derives from the Arabic root m-r-d, meaning 'recalcitrant' or 'rebellious'. The Quran in Surah As-Saffat (37:7) describes stars adorning the lowest heaven and protecting it from 'every rebellious devil' (kulli shayṭānin māridin), establishing marids as entities that attempt to eavesdrop on heavenly affairs. Later Arabic theological and lexicographical tradition (e.g. Lisan al-Arab, Hans Wehr) treats marids as a distinct category of jinn, often paired or contrasted with the ʿifrīt: in the schema reported by some Maliki Athari scholars (such as Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr), the marid is more malevolent than an ordinary shayṭān but less powerful than an ʿifrīt; other sources reverse the ordering. In the MacNaghten edition of One Thousand and One Nights (e.g. The Story of the Fisherman and the Jinni), 'marid' and 'ifrit' are used somewhat interchangeably. Western popular culture, drawing on these stories and on later occult writing, has often pictured Marids as wish-granting water-spirits bound to vessels — a partial but distorted reflection of the Arabic tradition.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Arabic active participle of the root m-r-d ('to rebel, be recalcitrant'); cognate with Hebrew מָרַד (māraḏ, 'to rebel') and Aramaic forms.
- **Meaning**: 'Rebellious one'; in Islamic usage, a powerful, rebellious jinn or devil.
- **First use**: Quran (mid-7th century CE), Surah As-Saffat 37:7.
